Registration for NEST UI 2026 runs through this: public pages, the sign-up
flow, and a dashboard for each team that registers. It is live at
[nestui.id](https://nestui.id), built on Next.js and TypeScript with Supabase
behind it, and it has taken more than fifty teams through registration.

## Two products wearing one set of components

My part was the front-end: the shared component library and the responsive
layouts on both sides of the login. That sounds like one job and is really two,
because a public marketing page and a team dashboard want opposite things from
the same button. The public side wants it large, spaced, and persuasive. The
dashboard wants it small, dense, and repeated forty times in a table.

Building those as one library rather than two is the only way the site stays
coherent as it grows, and it is also the thing most likely to go wrong: a
component that tries to serve both ends up serving neither. The layouts are
responsive on both sides, which on the dashboard means deciding what a table
does when it runs out of horizontal room.

## The hard part was who can see what

The interesting problem here was not load and it was not layout. It was
permissions.

A team has members. Members submit things. Any of them can come back later and
change something. So every read and every write has to answer a question first:
is this person on this team? Get that wrong in the obvious direction and someone
sees another team's submission — which, on a registration platform, is the one
failure you cannot apologise your way out of.

That constraint shapes the front-end more than it looks like it should. Every
dashboard view has to assume it might be rendering for someone who should not
see it, and fail closed rather than open.

## It shipped and it held

No launch-day story here: it went smoothly. Registration opened, teams came
through, and nothing needed fixing at two in the morning. Server-rendered pages,
image optimisation, and the SEO and Open Graph metadata were all in place before
it went live rather than after.

That is a less exciting write-up than a war story, and it is the honest one.
